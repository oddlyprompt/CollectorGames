# Rift Parade: will the metagame move?

Generated by `node tools/rift-parade/meta.js` and `meta_report.js` on 2026-10-19. 40 simulated weeks, every game played by the engine (balanced pilots; each pairing of decks is played 6 times and the result reused). **This is a model of player behaviour, not observed players:** the shares of the player population follow results with inertia and noise, and the builders are simple (test variants of popular decks, and random theme packages).

## The three worlds

| World | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Frozen** | Only the eight representative decks exist. Players shift toward whatever beats the field. Nobody builds anything new. (This is the "same eight decks forever" problem.) |
| **Discovery** | Each week two new decks are tried (60% a tuned variant of a popular deck, 40% a fresh theme package). A deck that holds up against the six most-played decks joins the field at 1.5% and can grow. Decks under 1.2% for six weeks leave. |
| **Release** | Like Discovery, but only episodes 1-9 are legal at first (91 of the 130 cards; the eight decks are repaired to fit). In week 20 episodes 10-12 (39 cards) are released and the full eight decks arrive. A stand-in for a new set, using cards that already exist. |

## What this says

**Yes, the metagame moves, and it keeps moving, if something keeps feeding it new decks.**

* With nobody inventing anything (Frozen) the field collapses onto about two or three decks: three different leaders over 40 weeks, one of them in charge for 18 weeks in a row, and the most-played deck averaging 59% of the field. That is the "same few decks forever" outcome.
* With builders testing new decks each week (Discovery), twice as many decks led (6.0 against 3.0), the field held 13-14 decks instead of 7, the most-played deck averaged 42% instead of 59%, and about 40 new decks were admitted per run. 12 of them reached the top five and 4 led the field. Leaders after week 20 included decks that did not exist at the start: new theme packages and tuned variants, not just the eight starting decks. The representative decks led for 40 of 40 frozen weeks but only 16 of 40 in the Discovery world.
* The release (episodes 10-12 becoming legal in week 20) was absorbed by the existing field: leaders changed once or twice in the eight weeks after, and new-card decks held 2-17% of the field eight weeks later. Deck lists were already churning, so a release behaves like one more source of new decks rather than a reset. This is a weak test: the "new" cards were already part of the balance work, and the field simply had no time to rebuild around them in 8 weeks.

## Warnings, not to be skipped

1. **The model over-concentrates.** Discovery run 1 ended with one variant at 71-79% of the field (effective number of decks 1.6-1.9). That is partly an artifact: players copy winners in my model with a strong, fast response (an exponential in the win-rate gap, with each matchup measured from only 6 games), and the decks admitted are selected for having looked good in small samples, so a few lucky matchups get amplified. A real population has netdeck lag, preferences and favourite characters. So the right reading is "the meta will turn over", not "a 70% monopoly will occur". But it does show that the engine can produce a single deck that beats the field by enough that, with a perfectly rational population, everyone converges on it. A hard cap on how fast shares move, and a rules-committee check (ban or nerf a deck above about 30% for several weeks, as Wildkin does), should be part of the design.
2. **Only two runs per world.** The averages above are suggestive, not tight; leaders varied from run to run.
3. **The builders are crude** (random mutation of popular decks and random theme packages, a deck admitted if it scores 47% or better against the six most-played decks in 6 games each). Real players are both cleverer and lazier.
4. **The model is of the pilot "balanced" for everybody.** Human skill spread would add variety.

## What to build into the game so the meta keeps evolving (planning notes)

These follow from the experiment and from the Wildkin experience; none is implemented, per the scope of this work.

1. **A weekly "brew" loop driven by the engine.** Each week, a few simulated players try variants (tech choices, a different partner theme, a new package of the same cards), test them with the engine against the most-played decks, and bring one if it holds up, then it plays slightly worse for its first weeks while it is tuned. `meta.js` is a working prototype of exactly this loop: `builders.js` has the generators, `lib.js` plays the games.
2. **Retire decks that nobody plays and make rediscovery slow**, so the field does not fill up with 30 decks (cap and fade rules are in `meta.js`).
3. **Make the card pool deck-flexible.** The persona experiment found that decks of unrelated themes and of 20-50% overlap with the starting decks can reach or beat the field. This comes from the design: there are no factions, so every card can go in every deck, and each character has several cards at different costs.
4. **New sets are data.** A new set adds cards with the same ability vocabulary; the existing 130 cards' themes (Mark, Wager, Impact, and so on) can gain partners. Any new op needs one function in `engine.js` and a card-audit entry. The release test shows the field absorbing new legal cards within weeks, and the builders already search the whole legal pool.
5. **Keep brakes in the plan:** a share-movement speed limit, a rules committee (ban or nerf by simulation the card whose loss hurts the dominant deck most) and rotation, because a rational population plus a slightly better deck equals a monopoly.
6. **Measure it in the live game** with the same numbers used here: different leaders per season, longest reign, effective number of decks, share of the top deck.
